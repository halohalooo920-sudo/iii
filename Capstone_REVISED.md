# Actual and Perceived Parental Educational Expectations: Expectation Discrepancy and Academic Stress among Chinese **[CHANGED: ~~University~~ → Senior Secondary]** Students

Parental educational expectations have long been recognized as an important factor shaping students' academic development. A large body of research has examined the effects of parental educational expectations on students' academic outcomes and psychological adjustment across different cultural contexts (Jeynes, 2024; Lu et al., 2021; Pinquart & Ebeling, 2020; Zhang & Yang, 2025). In societies where academic achievement and filial piety values are strongly emphasized (Cao et al., 2025; Li et al., 2020; Li & Xie, 2020), such as in many East Asian contexts, parental expectations often carry particular weight and may be more deeply internalized by **[CHANGED: ~~by students across developmental stages, including emerging adulthood~~ → students, particularly during late adolescence when academic transitions such as the Gaokao (National College Entrance Examination) make educational expectations especially salient]** (Zhang, 2024; Zhou et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2023). These findings have often highlighted parenting behaviors, such as parental involvement, resource provision, and encouragement, as the explanatory factors of how parental educational expectations influencing students (Davis-Kean, 2005; Jeynes, 2024; Pinquart & Ebeling, 2020).

However, existing studies do not always conceptualize and measure parental expectations in the same way. Some rely on parents' own reports of what they expect for their children (Li & Xie, 2020; Lu et al., 2021; Sakaki et al., 2025), while others assess students' perceptions of those expectations (Ma et al., 2018; Zhang & Yang, 2025; Zhou et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2023). Although both approaches are commonly used, they are not necessarily equivalent. Students respond not directly to what parents actually expect, but to what they believe their parents expect. Therefore, students' perceived parental expectations (PPE) may be more proximal to academic stress and psychological distress than parental actual expectations (PAE), particularly when perceived expectations are experienced as demanding, unrealistic, or difficult to meet (Bai et al., 2025; Fu et al., 2022; Zhou et al., 2025; Zhou et al., 2023).

Yet, treating PPE as a single predictor may obscure an important distinction. PPE may include two conceptually different components: one part may accurately reflect parents' actual expectations, and another part may reflect students' perceptual error or misinterpretation. In other words, a high PPE score may mean that parents truly hold high expectations, but it may also mean that students overestimate what their parents expect. These two situations may produce similar levels of perceived pressure, but they have different psychological and practical implications.

This distinction is central to the present study. If students experience academic stress because parents genuinely hold high expectations, the problem may be related to actual parental demands. However, if students experience stress because they perceive parental expectations as higher than parents actually intend, then stress may partly arise from misperception. In this case, the discrepancy between PAE and PPE may capture a specific "misperception component" that cannot be identified by PPE level alone.

Against this background, the present study examines the discrepancy between parental actual expectations (PAE) and students' perceived parental expectations (PPE), and investigates how this discrepancy is associated with students' academic stress. While previous research has shown that academic stress is closely linked with PPE, less is known about whether the discrepancy between PAE and PPE explains additional variance in stress beyond PPE alone. Moreover, although the literature on parental expectations has primarily focused on **[CHANGED: ~~adolescents in compulsory education (Fan & Hui, 2025; L. Zhang, 2024), comparatively less is known about emerging adults at the university stage—a developmental period in which parental expectations evolve in nature but remain highly influential~~ → younger adolescents in compulsory education or university students (Agliata & Renk, 2008; Fan & Hui, 2025; L. Zhang, 2024), comparatively less is known about senior secondary students—a developmental stage at which parental educational expectations are particularly intense due to the imminent pressure of high-stakes examinations such as the Gaokao]**. The present study addresses this gap. By distinguishing actual expectations from perceived expectations, this study aims to clarify whether students' academic stress is shaped solely by perceived demands, or whether it may be further intensified by misperceptions of those demands.

## Literature Findings and Theoretical Background

Existing research on parental educational expectations has documented both academic benefits and psychological costs, suggesting that the effects of such expectations are complex and not uniformly experienced across developmental stages.

Parental educational expectations are often regarded as a strong predictor of children's academic performance. Meta-analytic evidence shows a moderate positive association between parental expectations and academic achievement across different age groups and cultural contexts (Jeynes, 2024; Pinquart & Ebeling, 2020). These positive effects are often explained by family practices, as parents with higher expectations tend to provide more learning resources and a supportive academic environment (Davis-Kean, 2005).

However, the influence of parental expectations is not always beneficial. Increasing evidence suggests that expectations can also function as psychological costs, particularly when they are perceived as demanding or difficult to meet. Students who perceive high parental expectations often report higher levels of academic stress, burnout, and internalizing symptoms (Curran & Hill, 2022; Lu et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2023). They further suggest that this relationship may be non-linear, with excessively high perceived expectations becoming detrimental rather than motivating.

Taken together, these findings indicate that the effects of parental expectations are not uniform. Rather, they appear to depend on how such expectations are experienced by students. In particular, psychological outcomes such as academic stress are closely tied to students' subjective interpretations of parental expectations, rather than to expectations as reported by parents.

Among various psychological outcomes, academic stress is especially relevant in educational contexts because it reflects students' subjective appraisal of academic demands and performance pressures. In the context of parental educational expectations, academic stress may arise when students perceive that they are expected to achieve standards that are difficult to meet. This makes academic stress a suitable outcome for examining whether discrepancies between actual and perceived parental expectations carry additional psychological significance.

In the Chinese context, parental educational expectations may be particularly salient. Academic success is often closely tied to family honor, social mobility, and future opportunity, while filial piety norms may encourage students to internalize parental expectations as personal obligations (Cao et al., 2025; L. Li et al., 2020; Lin & Wang, 2022; Ng et al., 2014; L. Zhang, 2024). Importantly, in the Chinese cultural context, parental educational expectations often **[CHANGED: ~~extend well beyond university admission, encompassing postgraduate study, civil service examinations, study-abroad plans, and career trajectories. This 'expectation continuation' phenomenon means that university students remain highly exposed to parental expectations, even though their daily contact with parents typically decreases~~ → center heavily on university admission and the type of institution students enter. For senior secondary students, expectations are concentrated on high-stakes outcomes such as the Gaokao score, university tier, and major selection. This 'expectation culmination' phenomenon means that senior secondary students face peak exposure to parental expectations, as the Gaokao represents a critical and largely irreversible life juncture in the Chinese educational system]**. Under such conditions, students may become especially sensitive to parental expectations and may rely heavily on their own interpretations of what parents expect. When these interpretations deviate from parents' actual expectations, expectation discrepancies may emerge and become psychologically consequential.

## Discrepancy Research: What Is Known and What Is Missing

As parents and students may not interpret educational expectations in the same way, increasing attention has been given to discrepancies between different types of expectations. Broadly, existing research can be understood as addressing three related issues: whether expectations between parents and students are aligned (expectation–expectation discrepancy), whether expectations are met by actual performance (expectation–performance discrepancy), and whether students accurately perceive their parents' expectations (actual–perceived discrepancy).

These perspectives offer useful ways to organize the literature, and empirical studies have largely examined discrepancies among three core constructs: parental actual expectations (PAE), students' perceived parental expectations (PPE), and students' self-educational expectations (SEE). It is also important to distinguish educational expectations from educational aspirations. Expectations refer to beliefs about what is likely to occur, whereas aspirations reflect desired or hoped-for outcomes (Yamamoto & Holloway, 2010; Marsh et al., 2023). Prior research suggests that discrepancies based on aspirations and expectations may have different, and sometimes opposite, associations with developmental outcomes (Lv et al., 2018). The present study focuses specifically on expectations because they are more directly linked to students' perceived academic demands and psychological pressure.

### Inconsistency between PAE and SEE

A substantial portion of the literature has focused on discrepancies between PAE and SEE, which have been linked to both academic and psychological outcomes. When parental expectations exceed students' own expectations, parents may provide greater academic support, which can benefit achievement, but may also introduce additional psychological pressure (Wang & Benner, 2014; Zhang & Jiang, 2024). In contrast, when students' own expectations exceed those of their parents, learning may be more strongly driven by intrinsic motivation and autonomy, which can be associated with more adaptive outcomes and lower burnout (Bai et al., 2025; Song et al., 2024). These findings suggest that discrepancies are not uniformly harmful, but may have both benefits and costs depending on their direction and underlying mechanisms.

### Inconsistency between PPE and SEE

Similarly, discrepancies between PPE and SEE have been consistently associated with psychological outcomes. Empirical evidence indicates that such discrepancies are strongly related to academic stress, emotional strain, and burnout, regardless of whether perceived expectations are higher or lower than students' own expectations (Fan & Hui, 2025; Gang et al., 2024; Zhang, 2024). Compared with discrepancies based on parental reports, those involving students' perceptions appear to have stronger and more consistent links to mental health. Crucially, Wang & Benner (2014) believe that these perception differences may have a more profound impact on the mental state of students than differences based on PAE. This perspective once again confirms the significant influence of students' perceptions—rather than parents' actual expectations on psychological strain, which is consistent with the view that distress arises when perceived external expectations do not align with internal self-standards based on identity-control theory (Fan & Hui, 2025).

Importantly, discrepancies based on parental reports and those based on students' perceptions may not only differ in strength, but also in direction. For instance, when PAE exceed SEE, this gap may be associated with higher academic achievement, whereas a similar discrepancy based on PPE is linked to poorer outcomes (Wang & Benner, 2014). This contrast goes beyond a simple difference in measurement and points to an important yet less examined question concerns the extent to which these perceptions accurately reflect parents' actual expectations. This directs attention to the discrepancy between parental actual expectations (PAE) and students' perceived parental expectations (PPE), which captures the accuracy of students' interpretations of parental expectations.

### Expectation–Performance Discrepancy

A related body of research has focused on whether students feel that they are living up to parental expectations. In this line of work, the central discrepancy is usually between perceived parental expectations and students' perceived self-performance, often conceptualized as living up to parental expectations (LPE). Wang and Heppner (2002) found that the degree to which Taiwanese college students felt they were living up to parental expectations was a stronger predictor of psychological distress than perceived parental expectations alone. Similarly, Agliata and Renk (2008) extended this logic by examining multiple discrepancies involving college students' perceived parental expectations, self-performance, and parents' actual reports.

This literature is useful because it shows that expectation-related distress may not be explained by expectation level alone. However, these studies mainly address whether students can meet expectations, rather than whether those expectations are accurately perceived in the first place. In this sense, they provide an important foundation for discrepancy-based thinking, but they do not fully address the PAE–PPE gap that is central to the present study.

### The Core Gap: Inconsistency between PAE and PPE

Despite its conceptual importance, the discrepancy between parental actual expectations and students' perceived parental expectations has received comparatively limited empirical attention. This form of discrepancy is distinct because it directly captures perception accuracy: whether students' understanding of parental expectations corresponds to what parents actually report.

Existing studies provide initial evidence that such discrepancies may exist and may have meaningful consequences. For example, college students may overestimate their parents' expectations, and greater inaccuracies in perception have been associated with lower self-worth and poorer adjustment (Agliata & Renk, 2008). Similarly, mismatches between parents' expectations and children's perceptions of those expectations have been linked to emotional distress and poorer academic outcomes (Do, 2005). However, this line of research remains limited in scope, with relatively little work conducted in **[CHANGED: ~~Chinese university samples specifically~~ → Chinese senior secondary school samples specifically]**.

This gap is important because PAE and PPE may represent qualitatively different processes. Parental actual expectations are often associated with supportive behaviors, resource provision, and academic involvement, whereas perceived parental expectations are more closely related to academic pressure and psychological strain. A potentially problematic situation may therefore be one in which students experience high perceived pressure without corresponding parental intentions, support, or clarification. In such cases, academic stress may not arise solely from perceived demands, but may be further intensified by misperceptions of those demands.

The present study therefore does not attempt to replace PPE as a predictor of academic stress. Rather, it examines whether the discrepancy between PAE and PPE explains additional variance in academic stress beyond PPE alone. This distinction is important because PPE captures the perceived level of parental demand, whereas PAE–PPE discrepancy captures the extent to which that perception deviates from parents' actual expectations. If discrepancy contributes to academic stress beyond PPE, this would suggest that misperception itself may carry additional psychological significance.

## Summary and The Current Study

Overall, the existing literature points to three key insights. First, parental educational expectations are consistently associated with students' academic achievement, often through parental investment and support (Davis-Kean, 2005; Jeynes, 2024; Pinquart & Ebeling, 2020). Second, psychological costs are more closely linked to students' perceived parental expectations, suggesting that students' subjective interpretations play a central role in shaping their stress and well-being (Fu et al., 2022; Lu et al., 2021; Wu et al., 2024; Zhou et al., 2023). Third, discrepancy research has primarily focused on inconsistencies involving students' self-expectations, with comparatively limited attention to the discrepancy between parental actual expectations and students' perceived parental expectations (Fan & Hui, 2025; Wang & Benner, 2014; Zhang, 2024).

Taken together, these findings suggest that parental expectations do not operate in a direct or uniform manner. Instead, their effects may be shaped by how they are interpreted by students, raising an important but underexplored question: to what extent do students' perceptions accurately reflect parents' actual expectations, and how might discrepancies between the two influence psychological outcomes?

Addressing this gap has both theoretical and practical significance. Theoretically, distinguishing between parental actual expectations (PAE) and perceived parental expectations (PPE) helps clarify whether student stress is primarily driven by external demands or by internalized interpretations of those demands. Practically, identifying whether stress arises from genuinely high expectations or from misperceptions has important implications for intervention, suggesting that efforts may need to focus not only on reducing expectations, but also on improving parent–child communication and students' interpretations.

Based on this, this paper proposes the following two questions:

## Research Questions and Hypotheses

RQ1. Does the discrepancy between parental actual expectations (PAE) and students' perceived parental expectations (PPE) predict academic stress among Chinese **[CHANGED: ~~university~~ → senior secondary]** students?

H1. A larger PAE–PPE discrepancy—particularly in the direction where PPE exceeds PAE—will be positively associated with academic stress.

RQ2. Does the PAE–PPE discrepancy explain variance in academic stress above and beyond what can be accounted for by PPE level alone?

H2. After controlling for PPE level, the PAE–PPE discrepancy will account for additional, statistically significant variance in academic stress.

### Analytic Plan

The analysis will proceed in several stages. First, descriptive statistics will be used to examine the distributions of PAE, PPE, academic stress, and discrepancy scores. This will show whether students generally tend to overestimate, underestimate, or accurately perceive parental expectations.

Second, bivariate correlations will be conducted to examine the associations among PAE, PPE, discrepancy scores, and academic stress.

Third, hierarchical regression analyses will be conducted to examine whether PAE–PPE discrepancy explains academic stress beyond PPE alone. In the first model, academic stress will be regressed on PPE and relevant covariates. In the second model, the directional discrepancy score will be added. A significant increase in explained variance would suggest that discrepancy provides additional explanatory value beyond students' perceived parental expectations.

As a supplementary analysis, polynomial regression with response surface analysis may be used to examine the joint effects of PAE and PPE without relying solely on difference scores. This approach allows for a more detailed examination of whether academic stress is highest under conditions of overestimation, underestimation, or matched expectations.
